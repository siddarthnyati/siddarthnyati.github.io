01 · The situation

A 4.5-million-document bottleneck, run entirely by people

Every patient starts with a document — a faxed referral, a prescription, a chart note. On this platform, roughly 4.5 million of them a year arrived as faxes, and medical admins keyed each one in by hand to start the patient and bill the order. Manual intake at that scale isn't just slow — it leaks money and risk from four directions at once:

  • 01High labor cost. Every patient is minutes of skilled admin time on pure data entry — a cost that scales linearly with volume and never goes away.
  • 02Compliance risk from errors. Hand-keying PHI invites mistakes, and in a regulated setting a mistake isn't a typo — it's an audit and a compliance exposure.
  • 03Intake inefficiency. Slow, manual onboarding delays the patient getting equipment and delays the supplier getting paid.
  • 04Lost revenue from missing documentation. Incomplete paperwork means claims that can't be billed — revenue that quietly falls through the cracks.

I saw a workflow that was expensive, error-prone, and perfectly shaped for machine learning: high-volume, structured-enough documents doing a repetitive extraction job. So I took it on — product ownership of an ML platform to automate it, from the problem statement to the launch.

03 · What the platform does

Fax in, patient and order out — with a human where it counts

The pipeline takes an unstructured document and turns it into two structured business objects: a patient record and an order. The model does the reading; a confidence threshold decides what a human still touches.

04 · What I owned

The product decisions that made it work

The data scientists built the model. I owned everything that decided whether the model became a product — what it extracts, where a human steps in, what "good enough to launch" means, and the trade-off that governs all of it.

Defined

The document types and the fields

What gets extracted and where each field populates — prioritizing the document types that start a patient and bill the order, because that's where the money and the risk concentrate.

Owned

The compliance requirement

PHI protected, every access audited. I owned the requirement; engineering built the security controls, and we validated them with the compliance team — not an afterthought, a launch gate.

Shaped

The pipeline and the human-review policy

When and where a reviewer steps in, keyed to the confidence threshold — the rule that decides which documents a human still verifies and which flow straight through.

Set

The success metrics

The accuracy bar required to launch, the automation rate, and time saved — defined up front and tracked every release, so "is it working?" had a number, not an opinion.

Decided

The core trade-off

Bias toward accuracy over automation — the call that shaped the threshold, the review policy, and the launch bar all at once.

◆ The judgment call

"I biased the system toward accuracy over automation. A missed automation just means a human still does that one — a cost. But a wrong billing field is a denied claim — it breaks the customer's revenue and our trust. So I set the confidence bar high and sent anything uncertain to a reviewer. I'd rather automate less and be right."

In a regulated domain the errors are not symmetric, so I spent automation to buy correctness.

06 · The metrics

What I tracked — and exactly how it was measured

Five numbers, three jobs: one for model quality, one for business value, and a guardrail that keeps the automation honest.

◆ Why the guardrail matters

Automation rate and error rate pull against each other — that tension is the accuracy-over-automation trade-off, made measurable. Raise the threshold and error rate falls but automation rate drops; lower it and you automate more but risk denied claims.

07 · Questions I get asked

The six that come up every time

QDid you build the model?
No. The data scientists built and trained the model. I owned the product: the document types and fields, the compliance requirement, the human-review policy, the success metrics, and the accuracy-over-automation trade-off.
QHow do you get to 95%, and is that good?
It is field-level extraction accuracy (correct fields ÷ total fields). Whether it is good depends on the field: for the billing fields that drive a claim, 95% was not good enough to auto-submit blind, which is exactly why the confidence threshold and human review exist. Accuracy sets the bar; the threshold decides what still gets a person.
QIt is PHI. How did you handle HIPAA?
As a launch gate, not a feature. PHI protected in transit and at rest, every access audited, validated with the compliance team. I set the requirement; engineering built the controls. In a regulated pipeline, "it works" and "it is compliant" are two different launch bars.
QWhy accuracy over automation? Isn't more automation the point?
Because the errors are not symmetric. A missed automation costs one person a few minutes. A wrong billing field costs a denied claim: the customer's revenue and our credibility. I would rather automate less and be right, and let the threshold move up as the model earns trust.
QHow was the time saved measured?
Average time to create a patient record and a purchase order, before and after: 30 minutes manual to about 5 with review. The savings figure is that time delta × loaded admin cost × volume, with the per-patient assumptions shown above.
QWhat happens when the model is wrong or unsure?
It never silently ships. Anything below the confidence threshold goes to a reviewer before it becomes a patient or an order, and the error rate on auto-processed documents is the guardrail I watched to keep the threshold honest.

What I would keep

Four things moved at once: labor cost, compliance risk, protected revenue and competitive position. The decision underneath all of them was one threshold: accuracy over automation, with a person on everything uncertain.
